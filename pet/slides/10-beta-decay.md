<h2>3 · ¹⁸F Decays — β⁺ Decay</h2>
<div class="eq lead" data-tex="{}^{18}_{\ 9}\mathrm{F}\ \longrightarrow\ {}^{18}_{\ 8}\mathrm{O}\ +\ e^{+}\ +\ \nu_e"></div>
<p class="ref">inside the nucleus: a proton becomes a neutron</p>
<div class="eq sm" data-tex="p\ \longrightarrow\ n\ +\ \underbrace{e^{+}}_{\text{positron}}\ +\ \underbrace{\nu_e}_{\text{neutrino}}"></div>
<p class="sub">half-life 109.8 min · about 97 % of ¹⁸F decays emit a positron</p>
<p class="takeaway">Each decay releases one positron — the antimatter partner of the electron.</p>

Note:
Fluorine-18 has one proton too many to be stable. It decays by beta-plus decay: a
proton in the nucleus turns into a neutron, emitting a positron and an electron
neutrino. Fluorine has 9 protons, so after the decay the atom has 8 — it has become
oxygen-18, a stable isotope.

The positron is the antimatter counterpart of the electron: same mass, positive
charge. The neutrino escapes without interacting and is never detected. About 97 % of
F-18 decays happen this way; the rest are electron capture, which emits no positron.

A nice detail: after the decay the fluorine on the trapped FDG-6-P has become oxygen,
so the molecule turns back into an ordinary sugar phosphate — but by then the signal
has already been sent.
