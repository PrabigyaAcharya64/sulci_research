<p class="takeaway">Lines pile up where the tracer is concentrated.</p>

Note:
The scanner is a ring of small detector blocks (scintillation crystals with
photodetectors) around the head. When a 511 keV photon hits a crystal it produces a
flash of light that is turned into an electronic pulse.

The scanner looks for coincidences: two detectors registering a 511 keV photon
within a very short timing window, a few nanoseconds. Two photons arriving together
almost certainly came from the same annihilation, so the annihilation must lie
somewhere on the straight line joining the two detectors. That line is the line of
response. A single event does not tell us where along the line it happened — only
which line.

Over a scan the scanner records many millions of these coincidences. Each one adds a
count to its line; regions with more trapped FDG produce more decays, so more lines
pass through them (the faint blue lines). Turning that collection of line counts into
an image is the reconstruction step. (Modern scanners also measure the small arrival
time difference — time of flight — to narrow down the position along the line.)
