<p class="takeaway">One decay → two gamma photons flying in opposite directions.</p>

Note:
The positron does not get far. It loses energy by bumping through the tissue and,
within about a millimetre for F-18, meets one of the countless electrons around it.
Matter and antimatter annihilate: both particles disappear and their mass becomes
energy, E = mc². Each has a rest energy of 511 keV, so the result is two gamma
photons of 511 keV each.

Because momentum must be conserved, the two photons leave in opposite directions —
back to back, about 180 degrees apart (to within a fraction of a degree). This is the
key to PET: a single straight line passes through the point where the annihilation
happened, and both photons travel along it.

Two physical limits come from this step: the scanner locates the annihilation, not
the decay, so the positron's short trip blurs the image slightly; and the photons are
very slightly non-collinear, which adds a little more blur.
