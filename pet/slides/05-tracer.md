<p class="sub">a short half-life means a low radiation dose — but ¹¹C must be made by a cyclotron on site</p>
<p class="takeaway">The molecule decides where it goes; the isotope lets us see it.</p>

Note:
A tracer has two parts. The molecule carries the biology: a glucose look-alike goes
wherever glucose is used; an amyloid-binding molecule sticks to plaques. The
radioactive atom carries the signal: it emits positrons, which is what the scanner
ultimately detects. The amount injected is tiny, so it does not change the biology
it is measuring — that is what "tracer" means.

The two isotopes used most in brain imaging:
- Fluorine-18, half-life about 110 minutes. Long enough to be produced at a cyclotron
  facility and shipped to hospitals. Used in FDG and in the clinical amyloid tracers.
- Carbon-11, half-life about 20 minutes. Carbon is in every organic molecule, so it can
  label a compound without changing its chemistry — but it decays so fast that the
  tracer must be made next to the scanner.
