<h2>What Is Positron Emission Tomography?</h2>
<p class="lead-text">A nuclear-medicine scan that images what tissue is <b>doing</b>. A small amount of a radioactive <b>tracer</b> is injected, and the scanner measures where in the body it collects.</p>
<div class="compare">
<div><b>MRI / CT</b>structure<br>what the tissue looks like</div>
<div><b>PET</b>function<br>metabolism, receptors, proteins</div>
</div>
<p class="takeaway">PET maps where a tracer goes — and therefore what the tissue is doing.</p>

Note:
PET is a functional imaging method. MRI and CT show anatomy — the shape of the
brain, the size of a tumour. PET shows biology: how much glucose a region is using,
whether a protein such as amyloid is present, how many receptors there are.

It works by injecting a tracer — a molecule the body handles in a known way, labelled
with a radioactive atom that emits positrons. The scanner never sees the molecule
itself; it detects the radiation produced when that atom decays, and reconstructs
where the decays happened. "Tomography" means the result is a set of slices — a 3D
image.

In practice PET is almost always combined with CT or MRI in the same session, so the
functional map can be laid over the anatomy.
