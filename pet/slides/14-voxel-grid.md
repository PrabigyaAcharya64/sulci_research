Note:
The output of reconstruction is a 3D array of voxels — volume pixels, typically a
couple of millimetres on a side — with each voxel holding the estimated radioactivity
concentration at that location. The image is usually viewed slice by slice, and the
values are often normalised by injected dose and body weight to give a standardised
uptake value (SUV), so scans can be compared.

The slice shown is an axial FDG PET image of a normal brain, displayed with a rainbow
colour scale: red is the highest uptake, then yellow and green, with blue and purple
the lowest. The grid drawn over it illustrates the idea of voxels; the real voxels
are finer than the drawn squares.

What to point out: the cortex — the outer ribbon of grey matter — and the deep grey
nuclei (basal ganglia and thalamus) are red, because neurons there use the most
glucose. White matter is green to blue, and the ventricles in the middle take up
almost nothing, so they are purple.

This voxel grid is what all later analysis works with: comparing regions, comparing
patients, or registering it to MRI anatomy.
