<p class="sub">carbon 2 carries <b>¹⁸F</b> instead of OH, so the next enzyme cannot use FDG-6-P — and that ¹⁸F is radioactive</p>
<p class="takeaway">Cells that use more glucose collect more FDG.</p>

Note:
Glucose enters the cell through glucose transporters (GLUT) and is immediately
phosphorylated by hexokinase to glucose-6-phosphate, which then continues down
glycolysis to make energy.

FDG follows exactly the same first two steps — the transporter and hexokinase do not
notice the difference — and becomes FDG-6-phosphate. But the next enzyme in
glycolysis (phosphoglucose isomerase) needs the hydroxyl group on carbon 2, and in
FDG that position holds the fluorine. So the molecule cannot go any further. Being
phosphorylated, it is charged and cannot diffuse back out, and brain tissue has very
little of the enzyme that would remove the phosphate. FDG-6-P is "jammed" inside the
cell: this is called metabolic trapping.

The result is that FDG accumulates in proportion to how much glucose each cell is
using. And because the fluorine is fluorine-18, every trapped molecule is a small
radioactive source sitting exactly where glucose was consumed.
